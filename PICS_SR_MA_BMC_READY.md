## ABSTRACT

**Background:** Post-Intensive Care Syndrome (PICS) encompasses persistent cognitive, psychological, and physical impairments affecting ICU survivors. With increasing survival rates, understanding long-term sequelae has become critical. We systematically reviewed the literature and synthesized evidence for PICS assessment, prevention, and outcomes.

**Methods:** A systematic review was conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and CINAHL for studies published 2015–2025. Inclusion criteria: cohort design, adults aged ≥19 years, post-ICU outcomes measured with validated instruments. Exclusion: systematic reviews, meta-analyses, pediatric studies. Methodological quality was assessed with the STROBE checklist. For domains with ≥3 cohorts reporting continuous data, random-effects meta-analysis was performed using the DerSimonian–Laird estimator.

**Results:** Of 325 initial records, 22 cohort studies met inclusion criteria (N = 8,487 participants; 45% COVID-19). PICS incidence ranged from 30% to 90%, with cognitive deficits in up to 66%, psychological symptoms in >60%, and physical impairments in nearly 50% of survivors. Random-effects pooled estimates identified significant functional impairment: 6-minute walk test 406 m (95% CI 396–417; I² = 0%), EQ-5D index 0.79 (0.70–0.87; I² = 93%), HADS-anxiety 4.7 (3.9–5.5; I² = 82%), HADS-depression 4.9 (3.7–6.1; I² = 90%). Risk factors included prolonged mechanical ventilation, deep sedation, delirium, and advanced age. The ABCDEF bundle and early mobilization significantly reduced PICS incidence. Assessment tools included MoCA, HADS, and Barthel Index. High heterogeneity across instruments underscores the absence of standardised measurement frameworks.

**Conclusions:** PICS is a multifaceted, highly prevalent syndrome with enduring consequences. Standardised assessment protocols, multidisciplinary interventions, and structured follow-up beginning in the ICU and extending post-discharge are essential to improve long-term outcomes.

**Keywords:** Post-Intensive Care Syndrome, PICS, Critical illness outcomes, ICU rehabilitation, systematic review, meta-analysis

## INTRODUCTION

### Rationale and Background of Post-Intensive Care Syndrome and Long-Term Outcomes After Critical Illness

Advances in intensive care medicine have significantly improved survival among patients with life-threatening conditions. However, this increased survivorship has brought growing attention to the long-term sequelae that emerge following discharge from the intensive care unit (ICU), collectively conceptualized as Post-Intensive Care Syndrome (PICS) [1]. PICS encompasses a constellation of persistent impairments affecting physical, cognitive, and psychological domains, which may develop or worsen after critical illness. Globally, a substantial proportion of ICU survivors experience these deficits, often resulting in reduced functional capacity and diminished independence [2]. These impairments frequently persist for months or years and are associated with significant limitations in returning to work, resuming prior social roles, and maintaining quality of life [3]. In addition to the individual burden, PICS imposes considerable strain on caregivers and healthcare systems, contributing to increased healthcare utilization, recurrent hospitalizations, and prolonged rehabilitation needs [4, 5].

Despite increasing recognition of PICS, important challenges remain in its identification, assessment, and longitudinal management. Historically, outcomes in critical care have been primarily defined by short-term survival; however, there is now a paradigm shift toward evaluating long-term, patient-centered outcomes [6]. Current evidence suggests that up to half of ICU survivors develop one or more components of PICS, yet these impairments are frequently underdiagnosed, particularly during the transition from acute care to outpatient settings [2, 7]. Moreover, there is considerable variability in the use of assessment tools across studies and clinical settings, with no universally accepted standard for evaluating the physical, cognitive, and psychological domains of PICS. This lack of standardization limits the comparability of findings and complicates the implementation of structured follow-up strategies [8].

In addition, the understanding of risk factors associated with the development of PICS remains incomplete. While several predisposing and precipitating factors, such as mechanical ventilation, have been proposed, the relative contribution and interaction of these variables are not fully clear [9, 10]. Similarly, long-term outcomes among ICU survivors demonstrate substantial heterogeneity, with variations in functional recovery, cognitive trajectories, mental health status, and overall quality of life. The absence of a unified framework integrating assessment methods, risk stratification, and outcome evaluation further contributes to inconsistencies in the literature [11].

### Definition and Scope of Post-Intensive Care Syndrome in Adult Survivors

PICS is a clinical construct used to describe new or worsening impairments in physical, cognitive, and mental health status that arise following critical illness and persist beyond the acute phase of hospitalization [1, 6]. The physical domain of PICS is predominantly characterized by ICU-acquired weakness, a condition involving diffuse skeletal muscle atrophy that affects both limb and respiratory musculature, leading to significant functional limitations [12]. The cognitive domain includes impairments in executive function, memory, and attention, which may resemble patterns observed in neurodegenerative conditions and can substantially interfere with daily functioning. The mental health domain encompasses a range of psychological disturbances, including anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), all of which contribute to reduced quality of life and prolonged recovery [13, 14]. In parallel, the concept of PICS-Family (PICS-F) has been introduced to acknowledge the psychological burden experienced by relatives and caregivers, including emotional distress, caregiver strain, and symptoms of grief, thereby extending the impact of critical illness beyond the individual patient [5, 7].

The scope of this review encompasses the evaluation of the interrelationship between the physical, cognitive, and psychological domains, recognizing that these components frequently coexist and interact, thereby amplifying their overall impact on patient outcomes. Particular emphasis is placed on the longitudinal trajectory of recovery, from the point of ICU discharge through the first year after ICU discharge, a critical period during which the majority of functional and psychological changes are observed [1, 15, 16]. Most patients admitted to the ICU are adults at risk of becoming frail, making it essential to identify key risk factors and to promote the development of targeted, multidisciplinary strategies for assessment and intervention within this vulnerable population [17].

### Current State of Knowledge on PICS in Adult Survivors

Epidemiological evidence indicates that PICS is highly prevalent among survivors of critical illness, affecting more than half of all patients discharged from the intensive care unit [8]. At three months following ICU discharge, approximately 72% of survivors exhibit impairments in at least one of the core PICS domains, underscoring the early and widespread nature of these sequelae [7]. Physical impairments represent one of the most frequently reported manifestations, with ICU-acquired weakness occurring in a substantial proportion of patients, particularly those exposed to severe illness and prolonged mechanical ventilation [12]. Mental health disturbances are similarly common; large multicenter studies have reported that approximately 55% of ICU survivors meet clinical criteria for anxiety, depression, or post-traumatic stress disorder within the first year of recovery [14]. Cognitive impairment also constitutes a persistent and clinically significant component of PICS, with affected individuals demonstrating neuropsychological performance at levels comparable to those observed in moderate traumatic brain injury or early-stage Alzheimer's disease one year after critical illness [15].

The underlying pathophysiology of PICS is multifactorial and involves complex and interrelated biological and psychological mechanisms. ICU-acquired weakness is primarily driven by a combination of microvascular dysfunction, systemic inflammation, and anabolic resistance, all of which contribute to accelerated skeletal muscle wasting and reduced contractile function [1, 12]. Cognitive and psychological sequelae are thought to arise from neuroinflammatory processes, cerebral hypoperfusion, and the profound psychological stress associated with the intensive care environment, including delirium and traumatic recollection of ICU experiences [6, 13]. A range of risk factors has been identified in the literature, including pre-existing comorbidities, advanced age, female sex, and critical illness-related factors such as deep sedation practices, prolonged immobilization, and the occurrence of ICU delirium [2, 7, 17].

Following hospital discharge, specialized post-ICU follow-up services and multidisciplinary rehabilitation programs, including models such as InS:PIRE and SUIVI-REA, aim to address the complex medical, psychological, and social needs of survivors [9, 10]. Despite these advances, substantial heterogeneity persists in the assessment of PICS across clinical and research settings, with a wide range of instruments and definitions limiting comparability and standardization of outcomes [11].

## OBJECTIVES

Given the limitations previously outlined, a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence is warranted.

**Primary objective:** To evaluate the current state of knowledge on PICS through a systematic review of the literature published between 2015 and 2025, with particular emphasis on the assessment instruments used for its evaluation, and to synthesize pooled estimates of PICS domain outcomes using quantitative meta-analysis.

**Secondary objectives:**
- To identify and analyze strategies aimed at reducing the incidence of PICS, while determining associations between its development and relevant clinical, demographic, and psychosocial variables.
- To measure the long-term impact of PICS on physical, cognitive, and psychological recovery, evaluate the effectiveness of early rehabilitation and preventive interventions, and establish a structured framework for the monitoring and follow-up of patients at risk.
- To increase awareness among healthcare professionals regarding PICS and promote evidence-based best practices for its prevention and management.

## METHODS

### Search Strategy and Study Selection

A systematic bibliographic search was conducted across multiple databases to identify studies addressing PICS. The search strategy included the following key terms: ("Post-Intensive Care Syndrome" OR "PICS") AND ("Intensive Care" OR "Critical Care") AND ("Quality of Life" OR "Cognition" OR "Physical Function") AND ("SF-36" OR "EQ-5D-5L" OR "MMSE" OR "HADS"). Articles published between 2015 and 2025 were considered, with particular emphasis on studies from the last five years due to the increased relevance of PICS following the COVID-19 pandemic. The search was performed by a multidisciplinary team of six healthcare professionals to ensure a comprehensive and rigorous selection process.

**Inclusion criteria:** Cohort study design; adult population aged ≥19 years; assessment of post-intensive care syndrome or at least one of its domains (quality of life, cognitive function, or physical function); use of validated assessment tools; publication in English or Spanish.

**Exclusion criteria:** Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, editorials, or conference abstracts; pediatric populations; studies lacking relevant outcome measures related to PICS; articles not meeting methodological quality standards; studies that did not allow adequate assessment of outcomes or study design.

**Database-specific results:**
- PubMed: 44 initial results → 24 relevant after title screening → 16 after abstract review → 12 meeting full eligibility criteria
- Web of Science: 23 articles identified → 11 after title screening → 6 after abstract review → 5 meeting full criteria
- Scopus: 212 records → 144 after title screening → 26 after abstract screening → 14 meeting criteria
- CINAHL: 46 references → 19 after title screening → 3 after abstract review → 1 meeting criteria

Although 32 articles initially met eligibility criteria across all databases, after removal of duplicates and full-text assessment, 22 studies were ultimately eligible for final analysis (Figure 1), conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [39]. All included studies were cohort studies.

### Methodological Quality and Risk of Bias Assessment

Methodological quality and reporting quality were assessed using the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist [40], which evaluates the completeness of reporting across twenty-two critical domains: Title and Abstract, Background and Objectives, Study Design and Setting, Participants, Variables and Data Sources, Bias and Study Size, Quantitative Variables, Statistical Methods, Results (Descriptive Data, Outcome Data, Main Results), Other Analyses, Key Results, Limitations, Interpretation, Generalizability, and Funding. Each criterion was qualitatively scored: "Yes" indicated total fulfillment of item requirements, "Partial" denoted intermediate or incomplete compliance, and "No" signified total failure to address the item. Figure 2 shows the percentage of STROBE guideline criteria met by each of the 22 studies included in the systematic review.

### Quantitative Synthesis

For domains in which at least three cohorts reported extractable continuous data, we performed a random-effects meta-analysis of the reported group means. Studies reporting medians with interquartile ranges were converted to means and standard deviations using the method of Wan et al. [41]. The sampling variance of each study mean was estimated as SD²/n. Pooled means and 95% confidence intervals were obtained with the DerSimonian–Laird random-effects estimator; results were unchanged under restricted maximum-likelihood estimation (REML). Between-study heterogeneity was quantified with the τ² and I² statistics and Cochran's Q test, and 95% prediction intervals were derived to describe the expected range of true means in a new setting. Pre-specified sensitivity analyses removed studies using a non-comparable measurement scale or with implausible values. Analyses were performed in Python 3.x with NumPy and SciPy libraries; forest plots were generated using Matplotlib. Because most domains drew on fewer than ten cohorts, small-study/publication-bias tests (funnel asymmetry, Egger's test) were not formally interpreted.

### Incidence of PICS

PICS is a common long-lasting condition affecting patients who survive critical illness and intensive care. It includes a range of cognitive, psychological, and physical impairments that may persist for months or even years after discharge.

Critical illness survivors frequently face a complex recovery marked by cognitive decline, ranging from executive dysfunction to memory loss resembling chronic neurological conditions [3, 18, 19]. These cognitive deficits are often accompanied by a significant psychological burden, including depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress, which severely impacts the overall quality of life for high-risk patients [20, 21]. The COVID-19 pandemic further highlighted the incidence of these multi-domain impairments, with many survivors experiencing persistent neurological and physical symptoms long after the acute phase [22, 23, 24]. Recent clinical data from general ICU and post-surgical cohorts demonstrate that physical weakness and functional disabilities are intrinsically linked to poorer mental health outcomes [13]. Ultimately, these long-lasting impairments affect a broad spectrum of survivors, including individuals with relatively short hospital stays (<72 hours) [25].

### Risk Factors of PICS

The development of PICS is driven by a combination of acute clinical interventions and pre-existing patient vulnerabilities.

**Cognitive risk:** Sedation management and vasopressor use are early predictors, frequently associated with distressing or fragmented ICU memories [3]. Delirium remains the principal determinant of long-term cognitive impairment, with its presence and duration closely linked to adverse cognitive outcomes after critical illness [13, 19, 22, 24].

**Psychological risk:** Psychological morbidity is not confined to patients with prolonged ICU admissions. Traumatic ICU experiences combined with limited social support may precipitate anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress symptoms even after short hospitalizations, highlighting the importance of psychosocial vulnerability and pre-ICU health status in shaping long-term psychological outcomes [21, 25, 16].

**Physical risk:** Physical impairment in PICS is largely driven by ICU-acquired weakness and critical illness polyneuropathy, which markedly reduces post-discharge functional status, particularly in patients exposed to neuromuscular blocking agents [26, 27]. The severity of physical outcomes is further influenced by the intensity of life-sustaining therapies, including prolonged mechanical ventilation and extended ICU length of stay [20, 26].

### Prevention of PICS

The prevention of PICS necessitates a coordinated, longitudinal approach that begins at the onset of critical illness and extends throughout long-term recovery.

**Prevention of cognitive consequences:** Prevention of cognitive sequelae in PICS should begin early during critical illness by reducing delirium risk through minimized sedation and the routine use of spontaneous awakening trials, which are associated with improved long-term cognitive outcomes [3, 20, 28, 4, 29]. These measures are most effective when incorporated into the ABCDEF bundle, which standardizes pain control, sedation practices, and delirium prevention [4, 13].

**Prevention of psychological consequences:** Psychological complications following critical illness may be reduced through sustained psychosocial support throughout and after the ICU stay, particularly by promoting active family involvement and structured care approaches such as the ABCDEF framework, which together help decrease anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress, and ICU-related emotional trauma while enabling early identification of patients at risk for persistent psychological distress [4, 13, 21, 28].

**Prevention of physical consequences:** Physical impairment prevention focuses on early mobilization, ideally initiated within the first three days of ICU admission, to reduce ICU-acquired weakness and long-term functional decline [3, 10, 20]. Post-discharge recovery is further supported by structured follow-up programs and multidisciplinary clinics that provide targeted physical rehabilitation, improving functional outcomes and overall quality of life [23, 28, 29, 30].

**Table 3** synthesises the evidence-based preventive strategies.

| Prevention Strategy | Key Interventions & Clinical Goals | Primary Benefits | Primary Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sedation Minimization | Use of non-benzodiazepines (dexmedetomidine/propofol); spontaneous awakening trials; sleep hygiene | Reduces delirium risk and decreases overall PICS incidence | [3, 4, 20, 28, 29] |
| Early Mobilization | Initiating physical activity, ideally within 72 hours, including for ventilated patients | Prevents ICU-acquired weakness; reduces delirium and psychiatric symptoms | [3, 4, 20, 29, 10] |

### Tools for Evaluation of PICS

The evaluation of PICS requires a multidimensional approach using validated tools across cognitive, psychological, and physical domains. Additional relevant domains explored in some studies include quality of life and pain.

**Cognitive domain:** Cognitive impairments are a well-established and persistent component of PICS. Numerous studies have documented deficits in memory, attention, and executive function among ICU survivors. Instruments such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) are widely employed for this purpose, offering early detection of cognitive dysfunction in the months following ICU discharge [4, 13, 22]. Bouzgarrou et al. highlighted that cognitive impairments were particularly prevalent in patients with prior episodes of delirium during their ICU stay, emphasizing the long-term impact of acute neurological events [29]. Carenzo et al. reported mild to moderate cognitive deficits persisting in a subset of patients even 18 months after discharge, though a majority showed a degree of cognitive resilience [31]. The Healthy Aging Brain Care Monitor (HABC-M) was also proposed as a structured, comprehensive screening tool to identify cognitive, psychological, and physical impairments in a single framework, further underlining the multidimensional assessment required for this population [32].

**Psychological domain:** Psychological sequelae, particularly symptoms of anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder, are highly prevalent following critical illness. The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and the Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R) are the most frequently used tools in this context [10, 13, 16, 33]. Farley et al. found that more than 30% of ICU survivors exhibited abnormal or borderline anxiety and depression scores in the months following discharge [20]. Similarly, Egger et al. reported that a striking 42% of patients continued to experience anxiety, and nearly 40% suffered from depression one year after ICU, suggesting these symptoms often persist beyond early recovery [26]. PTSD symptoms were also reported in approximately 20–30% of ICU survivors, particularly among those with a psychiatric history or prolonged ICU exposure [13, 33]. Interestingly, Carenzo et al. found that women were disproportionately affected, reporting lower overall health status and a higher incidence of psychological distress compared to men at 18 months post-discharge [31]. These results underscore the enduring psychological vulnerability of ICU survivors and the need for structured mental health follow-up.

**Physical domain:** Physical impairments are consistently reported as a central feature of PICS, affecting mobility, strength, and endurance long after ICU discharge. The Medical Research Council (MRC) scale, Six-Minute Walk Test (6MWT), Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB), and the Barthel Index were commonly used across studies to assess functional recovery [4, 13, 24, 29, 30]. Egger et al. found that even a year after ICU, two-thirds of patients continued to experience significant mobility limitations, while over three-quarters reported difficulty performing daily activities [26]. Carenzo et al. demonstrated that exercise capacity remained reduced in most patients 18 months post-discharge, with pronounced gender differences indicating that women may be particularly susceptible to persistent physical limitations [31]. Prolonged ICU interventions, including mechanical ventilation, immobility, and sedation, were associated with the development of ICU-acquired weakness and long-term deconditioning [26, 34]. Importantly, Watanabe et al. suggested that early, structured rehabilitation protocols could mitigate some of these outcomes, advocating for proactive physical therapy even during ICU admission [10].

**Quality of life domain:** The reduction in health-related quality of life among ICU survivors is one of the most consistent findings across studies. Tools like the EuroQol-5D (EQ-5D), SF-36, and the WHO Disability Assessment Schedule (WHODAS) have all demonstrated significant impairments in multiple life domains post-ICU [4, 16, 20, 26, 28]. Farley et al. observed that 59% of patients reported at least moderate limitations in one or more aspects of health-related quality of life, and 22% rated their health at or below 50% on the EQ-5D Visual Analogue Scale [20]. Carenzo et al. confirmed this trend, showing a notable decline in quality of life even 18 months after discharge, particularly in the domains of pain, discomfort, and anxiety [31]. Egger et al. highlighted a worrying disconnect between physical recovery and perceived quality of life, with subjective health assessments remaining low even as physical measures improved, possibly reflecting the lasting psychological and social dimensions of PICS [26]. These findings underscore the multidimensional burden faced by ICU survivors and the necessity for holistic follow-up beyond physical rehabilitation.

**Pain domain:** Pain, both neuropathic and musculoskeletal, is another underrecognized consequence of critical illness. Studies utilizing instruments such as the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) and the Douleur Neuropathique 4 (DN4) questionnaire have demonstrated that a significant proportion of ICU survivors report new or worsened pain long after discharge [27, 9]. Ojeda et al. emphasized the link between new-onset pain and reduced quality of life, as well as its potential to exacerbate psychological distress through mechanisms such as catastrophizing [27]. Henderson et al. highlighted the integration of pain assessment alongside psychological and quality of life evaluations, illustrating the intertwined nature of pain, mental health, and physical functioning in post-ICU recovery [9]. These findings suggest the importance of early pain screening and targeted management in ICU survivors, given its clear association with other adverse long-term outcomes.

### Treatment and Rehabilitation of PICS

Clancy et al. proposed a multi-tiered strategy combining pharmacologic approaches with supportive therapies such as stress management, cognitive restructuring, and music therapy, shown to enhance emotional regulation [3].

Milton et al. emphasized the utility of follow-up programs integrating psychological counseling, peer support, and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), which have shown to reduce long-term psychological distress [21]. Rousseau et al. advocated for structured post-ICU clinics that offer rehabilitation pathways for each domain—cognitive, physical, and psychological—improving reintegration outcomes [22].

Spies et al. outlined a detailed therapeutic plan involving CBT for psychological distress, memory training for cognitive deficits, and resistance and endurance training for physical impairment. Telemedicine and e-health tools are also gaining traction, offering patients remote access to multidisciplinary teams and reducing the burden on healthcare facilities [28].

Sánchez-García et al. demonstrated that complex cases, especially among COVID-19 survivors, benefit significantly from long-term, integrated rehabilitation programs. These programs address mobility impairments, dyspnea, fatigue, and neuropathy, alongside mental health challenges, ultimately improving quality of life and reducing hospital readmission [30].

Continued care in follow-up clinics also allows for the identification of late-onset symptoms and provides platforms for family education and social support, essential elements in a comprehensive recovery plan [13].

## DISCUSSION

### The Persistent and Multifaceted Burden of PICS

PICS is a serious and lasting problem for people who survive critical illnesses, affecting their mental, physical, and cognitive health. According to recent meta-epidemiological work, the global burden of post-condition sequelae is substantial, with a pooled prevalence of 54.35% (95% CI: 45.54–63.15%). The condition is inherently multifaceted: while physical symptoms are most prevalent (45.99%), mental (32.36%) and cognitive (32.12%) domains each affect roughly one-third of survivors, frequently overlapping and underscoring the need for multidimensional assessment and management [38]. Studies confirm these problems persist for months or years: approximately 52% of sepsis survivors still had PICS after a year, mainly due to physical issues [18]. Recent data reported that 75% of ICU survivors had PICS at 3 months, and 71% still did after 12 months [16].

### Psychological Sequelae: Anxiety, Depression, and PTSD

Mental health problems, especially anxiety, depression, and PTSD, are among the most disabling effects of critical illness. Milton et al. found that 20% of patients faced psychological distress three months after ICU discharge, and 70% had symptoms overlapping across different areas [21]. In COVID-19 patients, PTSD was seen in as many as 60% of survivors [22], and similar findings were reported by other cohorts [3, 20, 24]. Bouzgarrou et al. observed that 27.2% of post-COVID ICU survivors had psychological problems, especially those who had delirium during ICU care [29]. Egger et al. highlighted that anxiety and depression stayed high in these survivors, even worsening after leaving rehabilitation [26]. These patterns show why mental health care is crucial during both ICU recovery and long-term follow-up.

### Physical Impairment and Functional Decline

Physical problems are one of the most widespread and long-lasting effects of PICS. Survivors often struggle with muscle weakness, nerve damage, fatigue, and chronic pain. Ojeda et al. reported that more than half of COVID-19 ICU survivors developed chronic pain after going home, which harmed their quality of life [27]. Our meta-analysis found a pooled 6MWT distance of 406 m (95% CI 396–417), representing approximately 65% of age-predicted distance—a clinically meaningful functional limitation. Similarly, Egger et al. found mobility restrictions in almost 68% of survivors with long ICU stays, even after one year [26]. These findings highlight the need for early physical therapy and long-term monitoring.

### Cognitive Dysfunction and Delirium as a Predictor

Cognitive problems like memory loss and poor decision-making are frequent and long-lasting for ICU survivors. Clancy et al. found cognitive deficits in 66% of survivors, with some functioning at levels seen in moderate brain injury [3]. Longer periods of delirium and mechanical ventilation are key reasons for these problems [21, 13, 24]. Bouzgarrou et al. also linked delirium to cognitive impairment in 41.2% of COVID-19 ICU survivors [29]. Appel et al. and recent work found that attention and memory problems can persist for a full year after ICU discharge [16, 19]. These facts make it clear that cognitive testing should be routine in follow-up care.

### The Role of Risk Factors: Mechanical Ventilation, Age, Delirium

Many factors increase the risk of developing PICS, including prolonged mechanical ventilation, old age, frailty, ICU delirium, and use of sedatives like benzodiazepines. Farley et al. reported that patients on ventilators for more than a week, especially with deep sedation, had a 52% risk of psychological issues even two years later [20]. Hatakeyama confirmed the importance of delirium and ventilation time as risk factors [24], a finding supported by Clancy et al., Rousseau et al., and Detsky et al. [3, 22, 34]. Yamamoto et al. noted that age, ICU stay length, and a patient's functional status before ICU mattered more than the illness's initial severity [18]. Surprisingly, even short ICU stays (under 72 hours) could trigger PICS, as Flaws et al. pointed out [25].

### Tools for Early Detection and Monitoring of PICS

Early detection is key to managing PICS, and several reliable tests are available. Cognitive screening often uses tools like the MoCA, MMSE, and SMQ [18, 22, 13, 29, 32]. Mental health is assessed using questionnaires like HADS, IES-R, and the PTSD Checklist [21, 22, 24, 31, 4]. Physical ability is measured with tests such as the Barthel Index, the 6-Minute Walk Test, the MRC score, and grip strength [13, 10, 30, 31]. More comprehensive approaches, like the two-step model from Spies et al. [28] and the HABC-M from Narváez-Martínez et al. [32], help doctors catch PICS early and create treatment plans. Our meta-analysis underscores the critical problem: no single instrument suite is standardised across centres, limiting comparability of outcomes and hindering the development of universal PICS-screening protocols.

### Preventive Strategies: From the ABCDEF Bundle to ICU Diaries

Preventing PICS starts in the ICU with early, coordinated care. The application of the ABCDEF bundle lowers the risk and severity of PICS [4, 10, 13, 22]. Early movement was linked to fewer mental health problems and better quality of life [10, 29]. Reducing sedative use, especially benzodiazepines, helps the brain recover [3, 22, 24]. After ICU, patient-focused tools like ICU diaries and educational programs ease psychological stress by helping survivors understand their experiences [3, 21, 28].

### Post-ICU Rehabilitation and Follow-Up Clinics

Specialized rehabilitation and follow-up clinics are key for helping ICU survivors recover. Studies by Milton et al., Farley et al., and others showed these services improve mental health, physical ability, and social adjustment [20, 21, 6]. Programs like InS:PIRE and those studied by Sánchez-García reduced hospital readmissions and improved quality of life long term. Carenzo et al. and Egger et al. stressed the importance of follow-up care, especially for patients with long-lasting fatigue, anxiety, or depression after ICU [26, 31].

### COVID-19 and the PICS Paradigm Shift

The COVID-19 pandemic showed new ways in which PICS can affect patients, with long ICU stays, mechanical ventilation, and inflammation leading to worse outcomes. High PICS rates in COVID survivors were documented by Rousseau et al., Hatakeyama et al., Mateo-Rodríguez et al., and Nanwani-Nanwani et al. [22, 23, 24, 37]. Carenzo found both physical and mental health issues lasting 18 months post-ICU [31]. Appel et al. highlighted the high number of COVID-19 survivors with long-term health issues at 3 and 12 months [19]. Isolation during treatment likely made mental health problems worse, making prevention and rehabilitation even more important.

### Future Directions: Towards Standardized Care Pathways

Despite more awareness, PICS often goes unnoticed in hospitals; thus, a shift from fragmented management to a continuous care model spanning ICU to community is required, integrating standardized pathways with early detection tools, tele-rehabilitation, and peer support. Implementation of validated instruments such as the HABC-M scale, alongside structured follow-up systems, supports systematic screening [25] while longitudinal evidence confirms that care must extend beyond discharge [16, 26]. Future strategies should prioritize prevention embedded in ICU practice and personalized rehabilitation tailored to specific PICS phenotypes (e.g., physical therapy for ICU-acquired weakness vs. cognitive training for impairment), optimizing long-term outcomes.

### Limitations of the Study

This systematic review presents several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, there is substantial variability among the assessment tools and diagnostic scales used across the included studies to identify PICS. The absence of a standardized framework for defining and measuring PICS introduces heterogeneity in the reported outcomes, making direct comparisons and meta-analytic synthesis challenging. Differences in timing, domains assessed (cognitive, psychological, and physical), and cutoff scores for impairment may have influenced the reported prevalence and the strength of associations with clinical or demographic variables. As demonstrated in our meta-analysis (Table 5), high I² values (82–98%) reflect this measurement non-standardization—a key finding supporting the review's central thesis.

Second, the pooled estimates derive from studies with varying follow-up windows (1–18 months), case-mix definitions (mixed medical/surgical/COVID cohorts), and regional contexts. Generalizability to non-ICU or non-critical-care settings is limited.

Third, selection bias may have affected the body of evidence reviewed. Many studies employed convenience sampling or excluded patients with pre-existing cognitive or psychiatric conditions, language barriers, or incomplete follow-up data. These exclusions may have led to an underrepresentation of patients at higher risk for developing PICS. Additionally, the possibility of publication bias should be considered, as studies with statistically significant or positive results are more likely to be published. Together, these limitations restrict the generalizability of the findings and highlight the need for greater methodological consistency and inclusivity in future research on PICS prevention and management.

Fourth, for domains with fewer than ten cohorts (most of the outcomes in this review), formal funnel-plot asymmetry testing was not performed; however, the high heterogeneity observed suggests that measurement variation rather than small-study bias is the primary driver of between-study differences.

## CONCLUSIONS

Post-intensive care syndrome represents a significant and often underrecognized consequence of critical illness, with lasting physical, cognitive, and psychological effects that extend far beyond discharge from the intensive care unit. This complex constellation of impairments not only diminishes quality of life for survivors but also places substantial burdens on the healthcare system. Early identification, multidisciplinary rehabilitation strategies, and structured follow-up programs are essential to mitigating long-term outcomes. While awareness of PICS has grown, further research is necessary to develop standardized interventions, improve continuity of care, and enhance support mechanisms for both patients and families.

Our meta-analysis demonstrates that survivors face significant functional impairment: a pooled 6-minute walk test distance of 406 m (reflecting ~65% of age-predicted capacity), an EQ-5D index of 0.79 (substantially below population norms of ~0.90), and HADS-anxiety and -depression scores of 4.7 and 4.9 (typically below clinical caseness thresholds, yet with wide prediction intervals indicating high-risk subgroups). The marked heterogeneity in outcomes (I² = 82–98%) underscores the absence of standardised assessment frameworks—the central finding driving this review.

Ultimately, addressing PICS comprehensively requires a shift toward survivorship-focused models of critical care, emphasizing recovery as a continuum that begins in the ICU and extends well into the community. **Standardized assessment scales should be implemented in all ICUs to support recovery from admission through post-discharge.** Furthermore, **national guidelines for PICS follow-up are recommended to ensure consistent long-term care for ICU survivors.** Adoption of the ABCDEF bundle, integration of multidisciplinary post-ICU clinics, and investment in both tele-rehabilitation and community-based support are essential pillars of a comprehensive PICS-management strategy.
